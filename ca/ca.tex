\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This project develops CRAG, an instructor-governed, course-grounded AI tutoring platform, to improve educational assistance in higher-level education, while studying student reasoning using the Holistic Cognitive Development (HCD) Framework. It extends an existing student-built system built by a previous batch of NUS School of Computing students. The original platform already supported course material retrieval, separate student and instructor management dashboards, pedagogical learning modes, quiz generation, and Discord extensions. After the project was handed over, the development focused more on making the platform more intuitive to compete with existing AI study tools such as Gemini Notebook. This included strengthening the connection of course evidence with chatbot outputs, proper conversational support, and study activities such as flashcards and quiz generation services.

The core problem when it comes to generative AI outputs is that a fluent answer is unable to show establishment of factual support, or allow instructor intervention when the answer lacks correctness. CRAG, therefore, combines course-scoped retrieval with bounded corrective search, answer attribution review and explicit answer verification by human instructors.

Progress made so far comprises an extended integrated platform, integration of inline citations into responses, a custom document embedding flow, consented external search, corrective search when coverage is weak, learning mode refactoring and auto-switching, student notes auditing, dedicated student inboxes containing instructor feedback, and a successful publication of a final camera-ready manuscript for IEEE TALE as a short paper.

For the next semester, from January--May 2027, research and development will draw on post-use feedback from students participating in the current semester's pilot test. Planned development includes prioritising reproducible answer-quality comparisons, assessing of source freshness and failure recovery, validation of dialogue coding with reference to HCD, and a feasible consented study of reasoning and revision to further examine how students explain ideas, critique responses, and justify reasoning.

\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\section{Problem and intended contribution}
In higher education, course content varies significantly across academic institutions in its scope and assumptions. This is mainly a result of the unique expertise and strengths of its teaching staff. Because of this discrepancy, general-purpose AI assistants will not take this variance into account, and will end up overlooking specialised content and omitting course assumptions. This makes them produce plausible but inaccurate explanations that will likely differ from the course material being taught.

As such, university students require course-specific assistance via a platform that grounds explanations in teaching materials and supports course instructor clarification whenever answers remain uncertain. The platform should also allow students to receive notifications when instructors review or correct answers, helping them revisit explanations that have been verified. Evaluating this workflow would require the effort and consistency of instructor reviews.

This project extends CRAG, an existing educational AI platform that was built by an earlier cohort of NUS School of Computing (SoC) students. The previous system featured course material retrieval, student and instructor dashboards, pedagogical learning modes, quiz generation, and Discord integration. Now, the present work aims to further develop this existing foundation into a more coherent study environment, where students are able to trace the claims and citations of an AI-generated explanation all the way down to the retrieved course evidence. To stay within the standards of similar educational AI tools, CRAG should also be able to continue conversations, allow practice on course topics, and have fallback procedures to ensure a fluid user experience, especially when evidence retrieval is weak. Reproducible comparisons of retrieval relevance, answer coverage, and citation support will help assess whether the current system meets these aims.

The development direction takes feedback from a pre-study survey, where it aligns with the convenience that students expect from established AI study tools. To remain competitive with similar educational tools, such as Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM), CRAG's usability is strategically benchmarked against them. As of now, CRAG's key differentiating factors from these tools include proper course grounding, instructor intervention, and a topic mastery system. Separately, post-use feedback from the current semester's pilot will help identify difficulties and prioritise further improvements.

Additionally, drawing on the Holistic Cognitive Development (HCD) framework, we would also like to find out if structuring human-AI interactions around iterative rounds of thinking, criticising, and reflecting, by manipulating AI-augmented feedback instructions, can deepen the feedback quality and reflective depth to better support learner autonomy. This requires examining how students explain their ideas and critique responses.

The supplied IEEE TALE paper describes this platform and reports a CS3103 pre-use needs assessment alongside earlier prototype evidence~\parencite{cragpaper}. This report extends that account with further implementation progress and a proposed investigation for the next semester.

\section{Objectives and completion criteria}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Improve course-grounded assistance:} Develop a retrieval and response workflow that correctly retrieves content relevant to the question, and exposes the material to the user via citations and a source list. Evaluate whether the claims made in the responses remain consistent with the retrieved evidence.
\item \textbf{Make instructor governance easy and effective:} Streamline platform support of instructor reviews and improve the communication of review updates to students. Work with instructors to reduce the friction and overhead of this reviewing process. Evaluate whether the overall review workflow produces a measurable improvement in answer quality, and whether students are able to act on the feedback.
\item \textbf{Reduce friction in using the platform:} Improve the overall student experience across CRAG conversations, external integrations, and study activities (quizzes, flashcards, and notes). Review post-use survey responses from students involved in the current semester's pilot to get a general consensus of their experiences with CRAG, perceived benefits, and common pain points. Use the feedback to drive platform improvements.
\item \textbf{Support meaningful student interaction:} Connect and integrate the various learning modes, so that students have more opportunities to explain and refine their understanding. 
\item \textbf{Evaluate platform effectiveness:} Establish a proper analysis of CRAG using a fixed set of questions, identical course materials, and assessment criteria. Identify and record limitations and failures to assess whether the platform meets the intended objectives.
\end{enumerate}


\chapter{Literature review}
\section{Scope and organising argument}
This focused review examines retrieval and attribution, educational dialogue, instructor oversight and ethical learning analytics. It draws on primary research and the theoretical sources underlying the TALE paper, with additional work on corrective retrieval and contemporary educational assistants. It is a thematic review rather than an exhaustive systematic review: no complete database search or screening protocol is claimed. Its organising argument is that reliable educational assistance requires both inspectable evidence and opportunities for learners to exercise judgement.

\section{Retrieval improves access to knowledge but leaves support unresolved}
Lewis et al.\ combine parametric generation with retrieved non-parametric knowledge, demonstrating the value of retrieval for knowledge-intensive tasks~\parencite{lewis}. This provides a foundation for using course materials as an explicit evidence collection. However, improvements on open-domain benchmarks do not determine whether a particular lecture passage supports a course-specific explanation. In CRAG, the instructional context, document structure and question assumptions must still be preserved.

Corrective Retrieval Augmented Generation explicitly responds to unreliable retrieval through an evaluator and additional retrieval actions~\parencite{yan}. Its contribution is to make retrieval quality part of the generation process rather than assume the initial result set is adequate. The published approach includes web search and document refinement. CRAG adapts the broader corrective principle to an instructor-governed setting: course evidence is searched first, correction is bounded and external clarification requires student permission. This is an application-specific design rather than a replication of the published algorithm. Whether extra searches improve coverage enough to justify their latency and cost remains an empirical question.

These approaches leave an important distinction between retrieving a relevant passage and generating a supported claim. The ALCE benchmark evaluates fluency, correctness and citation quality separately, demonstrating remaining weaknesses in citation support~\parencite{gao}. CRAG's design consequently distinguishes cited evidence from additional retrieved context. A source link improves inspectability only when the associated claim is supported. Automated review may help enforce this contract, but its judgements still need independent assessment.

\section{Educational assistants and the limits of capability comparisons}
Educational conversational systems already combine document access with course support. The ChatGPT-based Jill Watson uses a modular architecture and multiple documents, with classroom examples and comparative evaluation~\parencite{taneja}. This makes it a closer comparison for present-day course assistants than a description restricted to the original FAQ system. It also weakens any broad claim that course-grounded dialogue alone differentiates CRAG.

The useful comparison concerns responsibilities and evaluation. CRAG places instructor answer correction, approved-answer reuse, student updates, practice and research provenance within a shared course workflow. These design choices can be examined for their operational value, but a feature inventory cannot demonstrate better tutoring. Published results for another system also cannot predict CRAG's performance across different materials, models and learners. A defensible comparison therefore needs common questions, evidence and scoring criteria, with deployment configuration recorded.

\section{Self-explanation, practice and learner agency}
A self-explanation study found that prompting self-explanation supported deeper understanding of an expository text~\parencite{chi}. Their study provides a rationale for asking students to articulate reasoning instead of only presenting explanations. Its setting and intervention differ from voluntary university chatbot use, so it motivates a hypothesis for CRAG rather than establishes an effect of Socratic mode. A sequence of chatbot questions can still encourage superficial responses or frustrate a learner who needs direct clarification.

Retrieval-practice research provides a complementary basis for quizzes and scheduled review. Roediger and Karpicke show that taking memory tests can improve delayed retention~\parencite{roediger}. The instructional benefit depends on what learners retrieve and how outcomes are assessed. Generated quiz marks, self-rated flashcard recall and an internal scheduling estimate cannot substitute for an independent retention measure. CRAG's practice tools should therefore be evaluated for content validity before their educational effects are investigated.

The HCD framework organises learning around Thinking, Creating, Criticizing and Reflecting, supported by autonomy, ownership and scaffolding~\parencite{hcd}. It offers a process-oriented lens for examining how a student develops and revises an explanation. Its creative-computing context differs from course-question dialogue, requiring an explicit account of what each activity looks like in a transcript. In this project, the HCD cycle provides theoretical direction; the repository's L1--L6 cognitive-agency categories are a separate operational coding scheme requiring validation. Conversation length or an apparent increase in category cannot alone demonstrate cognitive development.

Taken together, these sources support a design in which learners can request explanation, attempt an answer, compare feedback with evidence and justify revisions. They also expose a tension: convenient answer delivery may reduce the effort that a learning activity is intended to elicit. Offering several modes preserves flexibility, while evaluation must examine the reasoning actually produced.

\section{Governance, security and responsible analytics}
Instructor approval introduces an accountable source of correction, but its scope must remain explicit. Approval of one answer does not automatically cover a similar question with a changed assumption, quantity or negation. CRAG's later exact-question and approved-alternative policy follows this reasoning: similarity can help retrieval, whereas transferring human approval requires a stricter decision.

Greshake et al.\ show how instructions embedded in retrieved data can redirect LLM-integrated applications~\parencite{greshake}. This challenges the assumption that selecting course documents makes their content safe to follow as instructions. CRAG therefore treats document excerpts and student attachments as data and enforces access and source identifiers outside generated prose. These boundaries reduce particular risks; adversarial testing remains necessary, including course-scope violations and academic misuse.

Ferguson identifies ethical challenges in learning analytics spanning consent, responsibility and the purposes of measurement~\parencite{ferguson}. These concerns matter when a tutoring record becomes research evidence. A student's course access, platform activity and identity linkage do not establish research consent. CRAG separates operational records from consent-eligible canonical research capture and restricts coding by study role. The research protocol must additionally specify withdrawal, retention and justified interpretation of derived labels.

\section{Research gap and design implications}
The reviewed work motivates three connected priorities: traceable course-grounded answers, an integrated study workflow informed by student feedback, and observable opportunities for explanation, critique and revision. Instructor correction supports these priorities by providing a route for addressing unreliable answers. The next phase will examine whether additional evidence processing improves answers, whether instructor review produces usable correction, and whether CRAG responses elicits meaningful reasoning and revision in follow-up student interactions.

\section{Custom document processing and embedding flow}
Document ingestion was revised into a custom processing and embedding flow around the existing Jina integration. PDF-to-Markdown processing retains headings, lists and tables for structure-aware chunking and course retrieval. Selected diagrams and visual regions can receive searchable descriptions. This processing and integration of course content aims to improve retrieval quality and preserve document structure as compared to undifferentiated text extraction.

Course setup also required more dependable failure handling. Onboarding completion uses database claims and atomic document commits, preserving staged retry inputs and preventing a failed completion from being recorded as successful. Provider calls have deadlines and bounded blocking concurrency. These changes address practical failure conditions such as long-running extraction, repeated completion requests and interrupted processing.

A durable ingestion worker was considered, but current document processing remains request-based. Keeping this architecture avoids introducing queue and worker operations before measured failures justify them. Ordinary uploads still lack an atomic replacement or general idempotency guarantee. The completed onboarding safeguards should therefore not be generalised to every document mutation.

\section{Conversational and corrective retrieval}
The query path now preserves original student wording and uses a bounded planner to propose complementary course searches. Results are deduplicated and combined by reciprocal-rank fusion. Coverage review considers the question and can trigger one additional search round for identified gaps. The current default evidence budget is five items, with bounded expansion when weak coverage identifies missing concepts.

This replaces reliance on a serial classifier/rephraser path with a more direct connection between student intent, conversation context and evidence coverage. Short supplied conversations retain complete user and assistant turns for planning and generation, helping interpret follow-ups that depend on an earlier distinction or constraint. Longer histories remain bounded. Explicit quiz requests take a typed tool path instead of being treated as ordinary retrieval questions.

The design aims to reduce unsupported refusals and incomplete answers while controlling model calls. Neither retrieval similarity nor the existence of an automated coverage judgement is treated as calibrated answer confidence. A coverage-review failure remains unknown evidence quality rather than permission to invent missing information.

\section{Answer attribution and controlled external clarification}
Generated answers now undergo a separate whole-answer attribution review against original evidence passages. The backend validates selected source identifiers and constructs citations, distinguishing validated cited material from other retrieved context. Bounded repair and regeneration paths handle malformed review output and rejected support; exhausted attempts return an evidence limitation instead of an unchecked answer.

This goes beyond displaying whatever passages retrieval returned. It aims to make the relationship between a claim and its evidence inspectable. The model reviewer remains fallible, and this implementation must be evaluated for both incorrect acceptance and unnecessary rejection.

External clarification adds a controlled response to recognised course-evidence gaps. Student permission is stored within an owned conversation and can be revoked. External findings remain distinguishable from course material; conflicting sources require explicit handling rather than silently overriding the course account. A clearly unrelated or retrieval-empty request does not automatically trigger unrestricted web search. This preserves course alignment while allowing students to request broader explanation where eligible.

\section{Instructor correction and safer reuse}
The web-answer review workflow distinguishes \emph{Confirm}, \emph{Clarify} and \emph{Dismiss}. Confirm approves the existing answer; Clarify supplies a reviewed answer and explanation. The first two create approved Q\&A and an in-app update for the affected student. Dismiss records the review without sending an inbox update. This extends instructor oversight into a visible correction loop rather than only maintaining a question bank.

Approved-answer reuse changed materially after the TALE implementation snapshot. The paper describes a similarity threshold of 0.85. Current code instead performs a course-scoped lookup for the exact approved question or an explicitly approved alternative wording before embedding. A changed premise, negation or constraint must not inherit approval merely because its vector is close to an approved question.

Eligible Standard-mode matches reuse the reviewed answer. Guided and Socratic modes generate reviewed adaptations from the approved pair, without labelling newly generated wording as verbatim human approval. Follow-ups and attached discussion contexts use their conversation-aware paths. Instructors can review alternative wordings manually; automatic draft-alternative generation is not implemented. These changes prioritise a defensible approval boundary, at the cost of fewer automatic reuse hits.

\section{Practice, follow-up and student study tools}
The inherited quizzes and learning modes were extended rather than newly introduced. Mode resolution now considers explicit or strongly implied learning requests while retaining instructor restrictions and a student automatic-switching preference. Quizzes gained further question support, persistent results and recovery from interrupted or duplicate submissions. Question attachments allow a student to open a contextual \emph{Ask CRAG} discussion without automatically sending a message.

Topic-mastery updates now use Bayesian Knowledge Tracing with an evidence cap, rather than equating aggregate accuracy with understanding. Correct and incorrect attempts update an operational estimate; limited evidence constrains its displayed value. This makes feedback more responsive while leaving parameter calibration and the relationship to independently assessed knowledge unresolved.

The supplementary-material rule is maintained throughout: topic-less uploads can inform chat, but quiz generation and selection exclude them. This supports broader explanation without silently expanding the practice curriculum.

October work added a notes-audit tool and private flashcards. The audit separates pasted notes into claims, expands terse terminology into retrieval queries and checks each finding against course evidence. Its outcomes distinguish support, conflict, missing qualification and evidence that is not established; insufficient retrieval does not mean a student claim is false.

Flashcards use topic-assigned course evidence, source snapshots and freshness checks. Their scheduled review uses an FSRS adapter, and changed or deleted evidence can pause affected cards. Flashcard ratings remain separate from quiz marks and mastery estimates. Both additions support further investigation of study behaviour; neither has established an effect on retention or independent understanding.

\section{Supporting access and reliability work}
API access now checks authenticated identity, course membership and resource ownership across the student web app, instructor dashboard and Discord integration. Staff/course grants remain separate from research and consent-administration permissions. This strengthens the shared service boundary as the platform supports more workflows and records. The contribution is the implemented policy and aligned clients; deployed role checks still require staging and live verification. Temporary development access must be retired before a named-account release.

\section{Foundations for an HCD-informed study}
Research infrastructure now distinguishes operational tutoring records from canonical research records. Eligible capture requires identity linkage, an active study and applicable consent. LMS operational inserts enqueue ID-only delivery intents for consent-gated, idempotent capture; Discord uses a separate transport path with reply lineage. Response provenance records conditions such as mode, evidence path and terminal status. These records support tracing what occurred, while hashes alone cannot reconstruct missing historical documents.

The instructor/research workspace supports operational L1--L6 labelling and separate canonical study coding. Canonical annotations use study grants, versioned codebooks and append-only revisions. Adjudication requires two independent current labels; changing an underlying label invalidates the effective adjudication. This makes disagreement reviewable, but does not itself establish coding reliability or validate the category scheme.

Reproducibility work includes generated hashed Python dependency locks, committed frontend locks and a root verification command covering dependency consistency, backend tests, shared frontend regression tests, frontend linting and builds. Disposable-database checks cover specified recovery behaviour. These are engineering checks, distinct from live provider quality, browser usability, complete migration execution or educational evaluation. No new application-test results are claimed from preparing this report.

\section{Provider and pedagogical choices}
The implementation moved generation from OpenAI to Gemini during August, while retaining Jina-based embedding integration. The engineering result is a revised provider integration, structured output handling and bounded failure behaviour. A commit or successful integration does not establish that one provider is more accurate or cost-effective. Future comparisons must record model versions, configurations and observed usage costs.

Similarly, direct, guided and Socratic interaction serve different immediate needs. Forcing every exchange into questioning could obstruct clarification; always supplying a solution could reduce opportunities for independent reasoning. Configurable modes and optional contextual discussions allow students to choose their activity. The proposed investigation will distinguish a feature's intended pedagogical role from what students actually do with it.

The next phase will use pilot feedback to guide improvements, evaluate the quality of CRAG's answers, and investigate student reasoning with reference to HCD. The research questions introduced earlier guide this work.

First, post-use feedback from the current semester's pilot will be reviewed alongside the earlier needs assessment. The next stage will use current-semester post-use feedback to prioritise improvements, compare answer quality reproducibly and assess source freshness and failure recovery. Dialogue-coding validation will support a feasible consented HCD-informed study of how students explain ideas, critique responses and justify revisions.
