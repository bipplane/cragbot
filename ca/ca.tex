\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This project further develops CRAG, an instructor-governed, course-grounded AI tutoring platform, to improve educational assistance in higher education, while studying student reasoning using the Holistic Cognitive Development (HCD) Framework. It extends an existing student-built system built by a previous batch of NUS School of Computing students. The original platform already supported course material retrieval, separate student and instructor management dashboards, pedagogical learning modes, quiz generation, and Discord extensions. After the project was handed over, the development focused more on making the platform more intuitive to compete with existing AI study tools such as Gemini Notebook. This included strengthening the connection of course evidence with chatbot outputs, proper conversational support, and study activities such as flashcards and quiz generation services.

The core problem when it comes to generative AI outputs is that a fluent answer is unable to show establishment of factual support, or allow instructor intervention when the answer lacks correctness. CRAG, therefore, combines course-scoped retrieval with bounded corrective search, answer attribution review and explicit answer verification by human instructors.

Progress made so far comprises an extended integrated platform, integration of inline citations into responses, a custom document embedding flow, consented external search, corrective search when coverage is weak, learning mode refactoring and auto-switching, student notes auditing, dedicated student inboxes containing instructor feedback, and a successful submission of a final camera-ready manuscript for IEEE TALE as a short paper.

For the next semester, from January--May 2027, research and development will draw on post-use feedback from students participating in the current semester's pilot test. Planned development includes prioritising reproducible answer-quality comparisons, assessing of source freshness and failure recovery, validation of dialogue coding with reference to HCD, and a feasible consented study of reasoning and revision to further examine how students explain ideas, critique responses, and justify reasoning.

\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\section{Problem and intended contribution}
In higher education, course content varies significantly across academic institutions in its scope and assumptions. This is mainly a result of the unique expertise and strengths of its teaching staff. Because of this discrepancy, general-purpose AI assistants will not take this variance into account, and will end up overlooking specialised content and omitting course assumptions. This makes them produce plausible but inaccurate explanations that will likely differ from the course material being taught.

As such, university students require course-specific assistance via a platform that grounds explanations in teaching materials and supports course instructor clarification whenever answers remain uncertain. The platform should also allow students to receive notifications when instructors review or correct answers, helping them revisit explanations that have been verified. Evaluating this workflow would require the effort and consistency of instructor reviews.

This project extends an earlier CRAG implementation built by NUS School of Computing (SoC) students. That foundation already included course retrieval, student chat, an instructor dashboard, pedagogical learning modes, quizzes and Discord integration. The present work aims to further develop this existing foundation into a more coherent study environment, where students are able to trace the claims and citations of an AI-generated explanation all the way down to the retrieved course evidence. To stay within the standards of similar educational AI tools, CRAG further develops its capabilities through stronger conversation handling, evidence review, practice discussions and student-facing instructor updates. Reproducible comparisons of retrieval relevance, answer coverage, and citation support will help assess whether the current system meets these aims.

The development direction takes feedback from a CS3103 pre-use survey, which motivates attention to follow-up questions, source inspection and instructor verification. Established tools such as Gemini Notebook, formerly NotebookLM~\parencite{googlenotebook}, provide useful usability references, but this report presents no formal competitor benchmark or evidence of feature uniqueness. CRAG's intended contribution is the integration of course access, instructor correction, practice and research workflows. Available post-use feedback will help prioritise improvements.

Additionally, drawing on the Holistic Cognitive Development (HCD) framework, we would also like to find out if structuring human-AI interactions around iterative rounds of thinking, criticising, and reflecting, can deepen the feedback quality and reflective depth of students to better support learner autonomy. This requires examining how students explain their ideas and critique responses with CRAG.

The supplied IEEE TALE paper describes this platform and reports a CS3103 pre-use needs assessment alongside earlier prototype evidence~\parencite{cragpaper}. This report extends that account with further implementation progress and a proposed investigation for the next semester.

\section{Objectives and completion criteria}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Improve course-grounded assistance:} Develop a retrieval and response workflow that correctly retrieves content relevant to the question, and exposes the material to the user via citations and a source list. Evaluate whether the claims made in the responses remain consistent with the retrieved evidence.
\item \textbf{Make instructor governance easy and effective:} Streamline platform support of instructor reviews and improve the communication of review updates to students. Work with instructors to reduce the friction and overhead of this reviewing process. Evaluate whether the overall review workflow produces a measurable improvement in answer quality, and whether students are able to act on the feedback.
\item \textbf{Reduce friction in using the platform:} Improve the overall student experience across CRAG conversations, external integrations, and study activities (quizzes, flashcards, and notes). Review post-use survey responses from students involved in the current semester's pilot to get a general consensus of their experiences with CRAG, perceived benefits, and common pain points. Use the feedback to drive platform improvements.
\item \textbf{Support meaningful student interaction:} Connect and integrate the various learning modes, so that students have more opportunities to explain and refine their understanding. 
\item \textbf{Evaluate platform effectiveness:} Establish a proper analysis of CRAG using a fixed set of questions, identical course materials, and assessment criteria. Identify and record limitations and failures to assess whether the platform meets the intended objectives.
\end{enumerate}


\chapter{Literature review}
\section{Retrieval improves access to knowledge but leaves support unresolved}
Lewis et al.\ showed that language models, when integrating internal knowledge with external databases, work better on knowledge-intensive tasks. This demonstrates the value of retrieval for tasks involving large amounts of content~\parencite{lewis}, and thus provides a foundation for using course materials as external sources. However, this does not guarantee a particular passage accurately supports an explanation, as the retrieval and response generation process still functions like a black box.

Corrective Retrieval Augmented Generation addresses this potential inaccuracy  by introducing an evaluator and additional retrieval actions to handle unreliable source text~\parencite{yan}. CRAG adapts the broader corrective principle to an instructor-governed setting: course evidence is searched first, correction is bounded, and any external clarification is only performed after student approval.

These approaches highlight an important distinction between retrieving a relevant passage and generating a supported claim. For instance, the ALCE benchmark evaluates fluency, correctness and citation quality separately, demonstrating weaknesses in citation support across language models~\parencite{gao}. CRAG's design consequently distinguishes cited evidence from supplementary context. It should be noted that while source links enhance inspectability, they only provide value if the associated claim is genuinely supported.

\section{Educational assistants and the limits of capability comparisons}
\section{Educational assistants and the limits of capability comparisons}
Some existing educational chatbots already ground their responses in course-specific documents. For example, the ChatGPT-based Jill Watson uses a modular architecture along with classroom materials to outperform traditional models in classroom trials~\parencite{taneja}. This makes it a closer comparison for modern-day educational assistants.

To effectively compare these systems, the focus should shift to system responsibilities and workflows. CRAG currently has instructor intervention via answer corrections, reuse of verified answers, and real-time student Q&A updates, all within a unified course workflow. These design choices thus allow us to examine each component for their effectiveness.

\section{Self-explanation, practice and learner agency}
A study found that prompting self-explanation supported deeper understanding of an expository text~\parencite{chi}. This result provides a rationale for asking students to articulate reasoning instead of only presenting explanations. However, a sequence of chatbot questions can still encourage superficial responses or frustrate a learner who wants a direct answer.

Retrieval-practice research gives a suitable basis for quizzes and flashcards review. Roediger and Karpicke show that taking memory tests can improve delayed retention~\parencite{roediger}. The instructional benefit depends on what learners retrieve and how outcomes are assessed. CRAG's practice tools should be evaluated for content validity before their educational effects are investigated.

The HCD framework organises learning around Thinking, Creating, Criticizing and Reflecting, supported by autonomy, ownership and scaffolding~\parencite{hcd}. It offers a process-oriented lens for examining how a student develops and revises an explanation. Its creative-computing context differs from course-question dialogue, requiring an explicit account of what each activity looks like in a transcript. In this project, the HCD cycle is grouped into L1--L6 cognitive-agency categories to determine the cognitive complexity of each interaction. This serves as a more accurate baseline as compared to using conversation length to measure depth of reasoning.

\section{Governance, security and responsible analytics}
In CRAG, instructor approval introduces an accountable source of correction.

Greshake et al.\ show how instructions embedded in retrieved data can redirect LLM-integrated applications~\parencite{greshake}. This challenges the assumption that selecting course documents makes their content safe to follow as instructions. CRAG therefore treats document excerpts and student attachments as data and enforces access and source identifiers outside generated prose.

Ferguson identifies ethical challenges in learning analytics spanning consent, responsibility and the purposes of measurement~\parencite{ferguson}. These concerns matter when a tutoring record becomes research evidence. As such, CRAG currently allows access only to students who understand and consented to the research implications.

\section{Research gap and design implications}
The reviewed work motivates three connected priorities: traceable course-grounded answers, an integrated study workflow informed by student feedback, and observable opportunities for explanation, critique and revision. Instructor correction supports these priorities by providing a route for addressing unreliable answers. The next phase will examine whether additional evidence processing improves answers, whether instructor review produces usable correction, and whether CRAG responses elicits meaningful reasoning and revision in follow-up student interactions.

\section{Custom document processing and embedding flow}
Document ingestion was revised into a custom processing and embedding flow around the existing Jina integration. PDF-to-Markdown processing retains headings, lists and tables for structure-aware chunking and course retrieval. Selected diagrams and visual regions can receive searchable descriptions. This processing and integration of course content aims to improve retrieval quality and preserve document structure as compared to undifferentiated text extraction.

Course setup also required more dependable failure handling. Onboarding completion uses database claims and atomic document commits, preserving staged retry inputs and preventing a failed completion from being recorded as successful. Provider calls have deadlines and bounded blocking concurrency. These changes address practical failure conditions such as long-running extraction, repeated completion requests and interrupted processing.

A durable ingestion worker was considered, but current document processing remains request-based. Keeping this architecture avoids introducing queue and worker operations before measured failures justify them. Ordinary uploads still lack an atomic replacement or general idempotency guarantee. The completed onboarding safeguards should therefore not be generalised to every document mutation.

\section{Conversational and corrective retrieval}
The query path now preserves original student wording and uses a bounded planner to propose complementary course searches. Results are deduplicated and combined by reciprocal-rank fusion. Coverage review considers the question and can trigger one additional search round for identified gaps. Retrieved evidence allows bounded expansion when weak coverage identifies missing concepts.

This replaces reliance on a serial classifier/rephraser path with a more direct connection between student intent, conversation context and evidence coverage. Short supplied conversations retain complete user and assistant turns for planning and generation, helping interpret follow-ups that depend on an earlier distinction or constraint. Longer histories remain bounded.

This design aims to reduce unsupported refusals and incomplete answers while controlling model calls for a better user experience. Coverage-review failure will return an evidence limitation, rather than return an unsupported answer.

\section{Answer attribution and controlled external clarification}
Generated answers now undergo a separate whole-answer attribution review against original evidence passages. The backend validates the source identifiers from a planned response and constructs citations, distinguishing validated cited material from other retrieved context. If validation fails, a bounded repair and regeneration paths will handle malformed review output and rejected support. If the answer still fails validation, CRAG returns an evidence limitation due to the lack of confidence in the planned answer.

This goes beyond displaying whatever passages retrieval returned by making the relationship between a claim and its evidence inspectable.

External clarification adds a controlled response to recognised course-evidence gaps. This helps to preserve course alignment while allowing students to request broader explanation of a question, which improves the user experience of the platform without outright rejecting the question due to insufficient course coverage. Their question would need to first be related to the overall course topic.

\section{Instructor correction and safer reuse}
The web-answer review workflow distinguishes \emph{Confirm}, \emph{Clarify} and \emph{Dismiss}. Confirm approves the existing answer; Clarify supplies a reviewed answer and explanation. The first two create approved Q\&A and an in-app update for the student.This extends instructor oversight into a visible correction loop, rather than only maintaining a question bank.

Approved-answer reuse previously used a similarity threshold of 0.85 to return verified answers. Current code now performs a course-scoped lookup for the exact approved question or an explicitly approved alternative wording before embedding, to avoid false reuse when questions are similar but not equivalent (e.g., ``What is a game engine?'' versus ``What is a game engine used for?'').

Eligible Standard-mode matches reuse the reviewed answer. Guided and Socratic modes generate reviewed adaptations from the approved pair, without labelling newly generated wording as verbatim human approval. These changes prioritise a defensible approval boundary, at the cost of fewer automatic reuse hits.

\section{Practice, follow-up and student study tools}
The inherited quizzes and learning modes were extended in the current implementation. Mode resolution now considers explicit or strongly implied learning requests to switch modes. Quizzes now have enhanced conversational support, and each question contains a contextual \emph{Ask CRAG} button to direct the student to ask CRAG about the explanation. This method also allows the student to input their thoughts and reasoning as to why they chose a particular answer.

A new notes-audit tool and private flashcards now form part of the student study tools, alongside the quiz generation. The audit tool specifically separates pasted notes into claims and checks each finding against course evidence. Its outcomes distinguish support, conflict, missing qualification and evidence that is not established. This allows students to identify gaps in their notes and revise them accordingly.

Flashcards use topic-assigned course evidence, source snapshots and freshness checks. Their scheduled review uses an FSRS adapter, and changed or deleted evidence can pause affected cards. Flashcard ratings remain separate from quiz marks and mastery estimates. Both additions support further investigation of study behaviour; neither has established an effect on retention or independent understanding.

Topic-mastery updates now use Bayesian Knowledge Tracing with an evidence cap, rather than equating aggregate accuracy with understanding. This helps with smoothening the mastery curve to make learning progress feel more representative of the student's understanding.



A supplementary-material rule specially allows for the inclusion of additional resources that are not part of the core curriculum. This allows for CRAG to reference these documents and provide responses, while excluding them from topic-mastery calculations and quiz generation.

\section{Supporting access and reliability work}
API access now checks authenticated identity, course membership and resource ownership across the student web app, instructor dashboard and Discord integration. Staff/course grants remain separate from research and consent-administration permissions. This strengthens the shared service boundary as the platform supports more workflows and records. The contribution is the implemented policy and aligned clients; deployed role checks still require staging and live verification. \section{Foundations for an HCD-informed study}
Research infrastructure now distinguishes operational tutoring records from canonical research records. Eligible capture requires identity linkage, an active study and applicable consent. LMS operational inserts enqueue ID-only delivery intents for consent-gated, idempotent capture; Discord uses a separate transport path with reply lineage. Response provenance records conditions such as mode, evidence path and terminal status. These records support tracing what occurred, while hashes alone cannot reconstruct missing historical documents.

The instructor/research workspace supports operational L1--L6 labelling and separate canonical study coding. Canonical annotations use study grants, versioned codebooks and append-only revisions. Adjudication requires two independent current labels; changing an underlying label invalidates the effective adjudication. This makes disagreement reviewable, but does not itself establish coding reliability or validate the category scheme.

Reproducibility work includes generated hashed Python dependency locks, committed frontend locks and a root verification command covering dependency consistency, backend tests, shared frontend regression tests, frontend linting and builds. Disposable-database checks cover specified recovery behaviour. These are engineering checks, distinct from live provider quality, browser usability, complete migration execution or educational evaluation. No new application-test results are claimed from preparing this report.

\section{Provider and pedagogical choices}
The implementation moved generation from OpenAI to Gemini, while retaining Jina-based embedding integration. This decision was made to streamline providers and keep costs low, with future implementations aiming to use Gemini embedding models to replace Jina. Tavily is also used in CRAG for external clarification and further retrieval.

Similarly, direct, guided and Socratic learning modes serve different immediate needs. Refinements were made towards improving the overall learning experience and response quality, to avoid always supplying a solution and reduce opportunities for independent reasoning. Moreover, the implemented autoswitching of modes (which can be toggled off by students) allows for flexibility in learning, where students can choose to receive more guidance or be challenged to think critically. Each mode will then be further evaluated for its quality and effectiveness in supporting student learning.

The next phase will use pilot feedback to guide improvements, evaluate the quality of CRAG's answers, and investigate student reasoning with reference to HCD. The research questions introduced earlier guide this work.
