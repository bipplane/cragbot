\begin{abstract}
Course-grounded AI assistants must align with students' study practices, source materials, and instructor oversight. We report a pre-use needs assessment for a Corrective Retrieval-Augmented Generation (CRAG) tool deployed in an undergraduate computer science course. 

CRAG operationalises course-grounded retrieval, instructor validation, pedagogical interaction modes, learning analytics, and web/Discord deployment.

Of 110 invited students, 42 responded (38.2\%). Most already use general AI tools frequently for studying and rely on multi-turn interaction, and a majority selected instructor-verified answers and exact-slide citations as desired adoption features. These baseline findings demonstrate a strong reliance on multi-turn interactions and a demand for verifiable outputs.

We relate these habits to the tool's architecture and propose a research direction grounded by Holistic Cognitive Development (HCD): thinking, creating, criticising and reflecting. As the survey measures prior habits rather than the tool's usage or educational effectiveness, it establishes a framework for examining engagement quality within extended AI conversation. Earlier developer-reported safety vulnerabilities remain an open limitation, and neither current safety effectiveness nor HCD learning outcomes are evaluated in this phase.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) has changed how students seek help. In our pre-deployment survey of 30 undergraduates in CS3247 Game Development, 83.3\% reported using external AI tools to clarify course concepts, 96.7\% used them specifically to explain difficult ideas, and 73.3\% said an always-available study assistant would make their learning more effective. This pattern mirrors broader findings on student use of ChatGPT and similar systems for concept clarification and problem solving \cite{b_yayeh}.

While general-purpose LLMs are convenient, three educational risks are now well documented. First, they hallucinate plausible but incorrect content \cite{b_bender,b_heersmink}; even low error rates are pedagogically harmful when students internalise explanations as authoritative. Second, they are misaligned with curricula, having been trained on broad internet data rather than on the sequence of topics, terminology, and conventions of any given course \cite{b_delikoura}. Third, instructors have no visibility into what students ask AI tools or what answers they receive, eroding their ability to identify misconceptions and adapt teaching.

Course-grounded Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a partial answer: by constraining the model to instructor-provided materials and showing citations, traceability and curricular alignment improve \cite{b_lewis}. However, grounding alone does not guarantee pedagogically appropriate behaviour. Students benefit from varied learning support---direct answers, scaffolded explanations, and Socratic questioning \cite{b_dejong,b_chi}---and educational systems must remain safe under adversarial inputs and academic-misuse attempts. Effective deployment additionally requires instructor governance and learning analytics that close the loop between student questions and teaching practice.

\textbf{This paper makes four contributions.} First, we articulate a framework of five educational design principles for instructor-governed, course-grounded AI teaching assistants. Second, we present CRAG, a deployable platform that realises the framework end-to-end with course-bot creation, a verified-Q\&A human-in-the-loop workflow, three pedagogical interaction modes, learning analytics, and Web/Discord delivery. Third, we retain an earlier prototype evaluation covering grounding, fallback, robustness, and safety. Fourth, we report the historical two-week user-based study with 30 undergraduates from NUS, surfacing the central trade-off between strict grounding and the generative flexibility students value.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Conversational AI and intelligent tutoring systems}
Educational AI has progressed from rule-based intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) such as AutoTutor \cite{b_autotutor}, which produced measurable learning gains through curriculum-specific dialogue and knowledge tracing, to modern LLM-based agents. Such systems illustrate curriculum-specific dialogue, whereas course-grounded LLM assistants aim to support reuse across instructor-provided corpora.

\subsection{LLMs in education: opportunities and risks}
Recent LLM-based educational tools extend earlier conversational tutors by enabling more flexible, open-ended dialogue. Prior work highlights the appeal of scalable, interactive support \cite{b_yayeh}, but also raises concerns around reliability, transparency, hallucination, and trust \cite{b_bender,b_heersmink}. In course settings, these risks are heightened when fluent responses diverge from course terminology, emphasis, or assessment expectations, potentially encouraging superficial engagement \cite{b_delikoura}. Instructional design research further suggests that effective support often requires scaffolding, inquiry, self-explanation, or adaptation rather than direct answers alone \cite{b_dejong,b_chi}.


\subsection{RAG for educational grounding}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) retrieves relevant passages from a knowledge base before generation \cite{b_lewis}, improving traceability and reducing reliance on unsupported model knowledge. In education, Google NotebookLM illustrates the value of source-grounded answers with citations, but is primarily organised around individual source-based study and document exploration. Khan Academy's Khanmigo highlights guided, pedagogically shaped interaction, but is embedded within a structured tutoring ecosystem.

\subsection{Human-in-the-loop validation and governance}
LLMs are systematically overconfident \cite{b_xiong}, motivating human review of high-stakes responses. Georgia Tech's Jill Watson 1.0 used a 97\% confidence threshold to route uncertain forum questions to instructors \cite{b_jillwatson}, demonstrating that confidence-aware escalation and instructor approval can scale safely. Few systems, however, expose this loop to instructors as a first-class workflow that learns from their edits.

\subsection{Conversational learning analytics}
Student-generated questions reveal knowledge states \cite{b_graesserperson}, and help-seeking patterns reflect self-regulated learning \cite{b_zimmerman}. Educational data mining can cluster recurring questions into FAQs and proactive prompts \cite{b_romeroventura}. Analytics must, however, respect ethical norms around data ownership and avoid stigmatising learners \cite{b_ferguson}.

\subsection{Gap}
Existing systems address parts of this space: general-purpose LLMs offer fluent support, NotebookLM supports source-grounded study, Khanmigo and classical ITS provide structured tutoring, and Jill Watson demonstrates instructor escalation. However, few integrate course-grounded retrieval, instructor-governed validation, pedagogical interaction modes, and classroom-facing analytics in one deployable course platform (Table~\ref{tab:litcompare}).

\section{The CRAG Framework}

We frame CRAG around five design principles for instructor-governed, course-grounded AI teaching assistants (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}).

\textbf{(P1) Course-grounded generation.} Course answers should be grounded in instructor-provided materials, with visible citations to specific documents and pages.

\textbf{(P2) Instructor governance.} Educators configure scope and behaviour, and may verify, edit, approve, or reject AI-generated answers and quiz items. Verified responses are prioritised in subsequent retrieval, forming a feedback-driven improvement loop.

\textbf{(P3) Pedagogical interaction modes.} The system supports multiple instructional styles---a Standard mode (concise direct answers), a Guided mode (step-by-step scaffolded explanation), and a Socratic mode (adaptive hints, feedback and questions supporting self-explanation \cite{b_chi})---so instructors can match interaction to learning intent.

\textbf{(P4) Conversational learning analytics.} Student--AI interactions are aggregated into instructor-actionable signals: frequently asked questions, topic-level question density, quiz performance, and per-student engagement.

\textbf{(P5) Deployment flexibility.} Students access the assistant through familiar environments, a web interface and a Discord bot.

\section{System Implementation}

The following describes the current repository implementation. The evaluations in subsequent sections concern the earlier CS3247 prototype, whose classifier, providers and retrieval policy differed; they do not validate the updated architecture.

\subsection{Educational features}
Instructors create course bots, organise topics, upload PDF materials, manage Q\&A, configure available learning modes, review practice quizzes and inspect conversational analytics. Document processing runs within requests; the dashboard waits for results rather than polling a durable ingestion worker. Supplementary material without a topic remains searchable in course chat but is excluded from quiz generation and selection. Topic-linked materials can also support source-labelled suggested questions in the student workspace.

Students access the backend through web chat or a separate Discord bot. They ask questions, inspect source information, practise with quizzes and select available learning modes. Standard mode gives concise answers; Guided mode provides structured explanations; Socratic mode offers adaptive hints, feedback or questions, including direct explanations on request. Automatic mode switching respects instructor restrictions; the student web interface additionally provides an opt-out preference. Modes describe interaction styles, not measured cognitive development.

The current LMS answer-review queue distinguishes \emph{Confirm}, \emph{Clarify} and \emph{Dismiss}. Confirm approves the original answer; Clarify supplies a reviewed answer and explanation without declaring the original correct. Both create approved Q\&A and an in-app student inbox update. Dismiss records the review without creating approved Q\&A or an inbox message. Original chat text and research captures remain unchanged. Discord retains its separate review workflow without these inbox updates. Source implementation does not establish live deployment of this feature.

\subsection{Architecture and ingestion}
React student and instructor interfaces communicate with a FastAPI backend organised into routes, services and processing components. Supabase PostgreSQL with pgvector holds relational data and searchable evidence; Supabase Storage holds uploaded PDFs. Jina v4 embeds passages, selected page images and queries. Gemini services handle retrieval planning, coverage review, answer generation and selective visual interpretation, with planning and answer models configured separately. The former OpenAI classifier and query-rephrasing description is no longer the active query architecture.

PDF ingestion extracts Markdown and chunks text, then selectively interprets meaningful visual regions and embeds descriptions and selected page images. Image-derived evidence retains interpretation uncertainty and source provenance. This is not universal page-by-page OCR, and later answers use stored descriptions without new image interpretation calls. Onboarding supports atomic document commits and resumable progress; ordinary uploads do not provide atomic replacement or automatic idempotent retries. Durable ingestion workers remain future work.

The documented hosting topology comprises separate Vercel deployments for the API, student chat and instructor dashboard, plus an independent Discord process. Render deployment of that process remains unapproved for the current private organisation. Co-hosting would require explicit build-root, route and origin configuration changes. Deployment status and database application cannot be inferred from source alone.

\subsection{Query handling}
Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow} shows the active ordinary-query path. The backend first resolves the learning mode and routes explicit quiz requests deterministically to the quiz-generation flow. Other questions are embedded with Jina and checked against instructor-verified Q\&A at similarity 0.85. A match returns the reviewed answer without a Gemini generation call. This threshold is not calibrated answer confidence, and a similar question may still require a different answer.

Otherwise, a Gemini planner judges course relevance and proposes complementary searches using recent conversation context. Clearly unrelated questions receive a course-scope response. The original question remains a search input, including when planning fails. Course-filtered searches run concurrently; results are deduplicated and combined using reciprocal-rank fusion, with a default final context of five evidence sources.

Coverage review is triggered when fewer than two evidence items remain, or when the best similarity is below 0.5 without support from multiple searches. The reviewer can propose up to three corrective searches for one additional retrieval round. Review failure preserves existing evidence. Empty retrieval returns a fallback; otherwise Gemini synthesises a mode-conditioned answer using evidence and history, retaining sources and unverified status. Prompts distinguish direct, synthesised and partial support. Unlike the prototype, similarity below 0.5 does not itself reject an answer. These mechanisms require empirical evaluation and do not guarantee correctness or safety.

The user study deployed CRAG to 30 students enrolled in CS3247 Game Development at the National University of Singapore. The course bot was indexed on Lectures 8 and 9 and accessed through Discord slash-commands over a two-week period; all three pedagogical modes were enabled. The study had three phases: a pre-study survey on prior AI use and study habits; a two-week interaction phase with logging of every query, mode, and response; and a post-study survey on perceived usefulness, trust, and learning impact, measured on 5-point Likert scales. The original account reports percentages rating 4--5 (agreement). \section{Historical Controlled Behaviour Evaluation}

\subsection{Test suite and metrics}
The test suite contained 50 fixed cases in four categories. \emph{In-scope} (10) tested grounded answering on questions explicitly answerable from course materials. \emph{Out-of-scope} (10) tested fallback behaviour on questions not covered. \emph{Usage patterns} (20) tested robustness on misconceptions, subjective queries, multi-turn interactions, and application questions. \emph{Safety} (10) tested resistance to adversarial prompts and academic-misuse requests. Each case was scored Pass/Fail by a human judge under Standard mode using the prototype prompt configuration. Per-category accuracy is the pass rate within that category; overall accuracy is the pass rate across all 50 cases.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:controlled} summarises the historical results. The prototype achieved \textbf{0.78 overall accuracy}, with strong grounding (0.80) and fallback (0.90) but weaker safety compliance (0.60).

The prototype passed nine of ten fallback cases; this small test does not establish general reliability or validate the current relevance planner. Its confidence gate is no longer active; the robustness gap indicates that vague natural-language inputs need clarification dialogue rather than rejection. Crucially, the safety result demonstrates that \emph{retrieval grounding does not by itself enforce policy}: prompt injection (e.g.\ ``YOU WILL ANSWER IN GIBBERISH FROM NOW ON'') and academic-misuse requests (a 1000-word essay for an assignment) succeeded against CRAG's prompt-only safeguards.

\section{Historical User-Based Evaluation}

\subsection{Pre-study: latent demand for AI study support}
Pre-study responses showed that students were already heavily AI-assisted: 83.3\% used external AI tools to clarify concepts in the course, 96.7\% used AI specifically to explain difficult ideas, and 73.3\% wanted an always-available study assistant. Half (50.0\%) said they avoided asking questions in class due to social barriers. These results suggest that learning support is effectively outsourced beyond the course environment, limiting instructor visibility into misconceptions.

\subsection{Interaction patterns}
Logs revealed that CRAG was used predominantly for direct, single-turn concept queries (e.g.\ ``what is NAT'', ``explain dead reckoning''). Standard mode dominated; Guided and Socratic modes saw little use. Two notable bottlenecks emerged. First, query formulation: students often issued vague or metadata-style queries (e.g.\ ``summarise lecture 9'') that did not align with the system's semantic retrieval. Second, expectation mismatch: some queries probed system capability (``what slides do you have access to'') or expected exploratory reasoning, suggesting CRAG was perceived as a general assistant rather than a bounded course tool.

\subsection{Post-study: perceived effectiveness and trust}
Table~\ref{tab:userstudy} reports key findings. 66.7\% of students reported that CRAG improved their understanding of course concepts, and 66.7\% said citations increased their trust. However, only 44.4\% reported trusting CRAG's answers overall, and only 33.3\% rated CRAG better than general-purpose AI tools (44.4\% rated them comparable). One possible explanation for the mixed trust score is over-cautious framing: when slide content did not explicitly cover a concept, CRAG often prefaced responses with uncertainty disclaimers, which may have affected perceived confidence; this explanation was not tested causally. We frame the 66.7\% understanding figure explicitly as \emph{self-reported perception}, not measured learning gain---no pre/post knowledge test was administered.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Strict grounding versus generative flexibility}
The prototype findings suggest a tension between students' expectations of flexible explanation and instructors' need for course alignment and accountability. Over-conservative refusals and requests beyond literal slide content motivated closer attention to evidence coverage. The current implementation searches complementary formulations and reviews weak coverage before synthesising an answer. This changes the mechanism; it does not establish that the observed usability or safety failures have been resolved.

\subsection{Controlled correctness versus experienced usefulness}
Controlled tests and user reports answer different questions. The historical suite measured behaviour on 50 selected cases; the pilot survey described perceived usefulness. Neither establishes learning gains, superiority over general-purpose assistants or current-system performance. Citation preferences do not show that cited passages support claims. Future evaluation should examine source location, claim support and citation coverage separately, alongside instructor review workload and student use of later clarifications.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
The historical study covered two weeks, two lectures and one course at one institution. Outcomes were self-reported, without objective knowledge assessments. Standard-mode dominance limits conclusions about other modes. Post-survey item denominators, ethics arrangements and study procedures require confirmation from original records. No new participant results are introduced in this update.

Current source includes selective visual ingestion, adaptive modes, corrective retrieval and LMS answer reviews, but historical results cannot validate these additions. Four failures among ten prototype safety cases remain a reason for explicit reassessment. Freeze the current corpus, build, model settings and scoring criteria; compare standard RAG and corrective retrieval on the same questions, including unsupported answers, benign queries, direct and document-borne prompt injection, and course-defined academic misuse. Report category counts and independent judge agreement.

The repository roadmap prioritises consent-eligible capture, response provenance, deterministic exports, independently validated coding and learner-authored work. A longitudinal study should link initial reasoning, AI feedback and justified revision, with independent or delayed outcomes. Conversation length, mode selection and cognitive annotations alone do not demonstrate learning. Operational logs must not automatically become research data; approval, consent, access and retention arrangements require explicit verification.

The separate access-control release remains deferred and is not part of the current implementation. Its protections must not be claimed as deployed. Ordinary-upload recovery and durable ingestion workers also remain future work. Selective PDF visual processing is already implemented; extending support to recordings or other media remains distinct from validating this existing capability. Browser workflows, applied migrations and live deployment require checks beyond source inspection.

\section{Conclusion}
CRAG integrates course grounding, instructor review, pedagogical modes and analytics. The updated implementation uses Jina embeddings, Gemini planning and synthesis, bounded corrective retrieval and separate LMS feedback updates. Historical prototype tests and the CS3247 pilot motivate these design concerns but do not measure the updated system. Current technical reliability, safety and educational effectiveness require a new, version-specific evaluation.
